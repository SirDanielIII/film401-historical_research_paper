1. INTRODUCTION
File: 01-introduction.mp3 | 73 words | About 0:31 at 140 wpm

In Mary Poppins, Dick Van Dyke dances with animated penguins. The performance and animation were made separately, then combined using a backdrop lit by yellow sodium-vapour lamps.

Petro Vlahos helped develop that system. He was an engineer who spent decades improving how filmmakers separated actors from their backgrounds. His work also included colour-difference blue-screen photography and, later, Ultimatte electronic compositing. Following his career shows how laboratory engineering became essential to familiar film effects.

2. AN ENGINEER ENTERS HOLLYWOOD
File: 02-biography.mp3 | 164 words | About 1:10 at 140 wpm

Vlahos was born in Raton, New Mexico, in 1916. His family moved to San Pedro, California, when he was eight. He graduated from Berkeley in 1941, worked at Douglas Aircraft during the Second World War, and later became a radar engineer at Bell Laboratories.

At Bell Labs, an MGM sound director introduced him to the Motion Picture Research Council, which coordinated technical work across studios. That connection brought him into filmmaking. After the council dissolved in 1960, he worked outside film before returning as chief scientist at the Motion Picture Research Center in 1968. The centre was operated by the Association of Motion Picture and Television Producers.

Hollywood was under pressure. Television drew audiences into their homes, and studios invested in widescreen spectacles to bring them back. The Paramount antitrust decision was changing the studio business, while HUAC investigations and blacklisting threatened film workers’ careers. Within this unsettled industry, shared laboratories gave engineers like Vlahos a place to develop tools for increasingly demanding productions.

3. BEFORE VLAHOS AND COLOUR DIFFERENCE
File: 03-colour-difference.mp3 | 164 words | About 1:10 at 140 wpm

Compositing already had a long history. Larry Butler’s blue-screen work on The Thief of Bagdad, released in 1940, came before Vlahos. A travelling matte is a mask that follows a moving subject from frame to frame, allowing the original background to be replaced.

The difficult part was preserving the boundary. Hair, glass and motion blur mix foreground and background light. A crude mask loses that detail and can leave a coloured outline.

MGM needed better composites for Ben-Hur, photographed in 65 millimetres. Vlahos’s existing sodium system used a modified 35-millimetre camera, so he developed a more flexible blue-screen approach.

His colour-difference method compared red, green and blue photographic records. The backing registered differently from most foreground subjects. Through carefully controlled printing, he used those differences to make mattes and reconstruct foreground colour with fewer blue fringes.

The RGB demonstration shows why comparing colour information helps. Vlahos worked with film separations and laboratory exposures. He filed his patent in 1959; it was granted in 1964.

4. SODIUM VAPOUR AND MARY POPPINS
File: 04-sodium-vapour.mp3 | 135 words | About 0:58 at 140 wpm

Vlahos had also been developing sodium-vapour photography, filing that patent in 1958. Here the separation happened inside the camera.

Sodium lamps illuminated the backdrop with narrowband yellow light near 589 nanometres. The actors received separate, ordinary lighting. A specially coated prism separated the sodium band from the rest of the scene light. Colour film recorded the performance, while black-and-white film recorded the matte at the same instant.

Those matched records helped preserve fine detail when Disney combined live actors with animation in Mary Poppins. Vlahos developed the prism system, and Ub Iwerks helped adapt the camera for Disney’s productions. Wadsworth E. Pohl shares their Academy technical award, presented in 1965.

It was a collaborative achievement. The specialized camera, lamps and optics also limited the system’s flexibility, helping explain why Vlahos continued working on other approaches.

5. ULTIMATTE AND LEGACY
File: 05-legacy.mp3 | 132 words | About 0:57 at 140 wpm

In 1976, Vlahos founded Ultimatte to develop electronic compositing equipment. His later patent addressed uneven screens, shadows, reflected blue light and partial transparency. Electronic circuits compared colour signals and combined foreground and background without the repeated printing stages of a film laboratory.

This extended his work into television production as well as film. He received an Emmy in 1978, and he and his son Paul received an Academy Award for the Ultimatte process in 1995.

Today’s digital keyers still have to separate subjects carefully and control unwanted backing colour. In 2024, Paul Debevec and Corridor Crew even revisited sodium-vapour matting with digital cameras.

Vlahos’s career connects several generations of filmmaking technology. His contribution was to make separately photographed elements easier to combine convincingly, giving production crews more control over the finished image.
