In 1976, Vlahos founded Ultimatte, extending his work into electronic compositing. Instead of constructing photographic records, electronic systems compared image signals. His patent addressed uneven screens, shadows, reflected blue light, and partial transparency. Later, he and his son Paul received an Academy Award for the Ultimatte process, for the 1994 awards year. His 1978 Emmy also recognized the importance of his work to television, alongside his contributions to motion-picture effects.

Modern digital tools still face those problems. A camera records colour, but it doesn’t automatically know where a subject ends. A matte still has to be derived, and the lighting and perspective still have to match.

Corridor Crew revisited sodium-vapour matting in 2024. That reconstruction shows continued interest in optical separation; it isn’t evidence that the old process suits every production. Across film and electronics, Vlahos helped make image separation more dependable and gave filmmakers more options for assembling a scene.
