What's really interesting is that Vlahos didn't stop with these photographic techniques. As technology developed, he continued looking for ways to improve compositing.

In 1976, he founded a company called Ultimatte, which brought his colour-difference techniques into electronic video.

Instead of relying on complicated film processing, Ultimatte used electronic video signals to separate subjects from their backgrounds. It helped make high-quality compositing more practical for television, such as weather presenters standing in front of weather maps.

His work earned him an Emmy in 1978, and he later shared another Academy Award with his son, Paul, for their work on Ultimatte.

Today, we have digital cameras and software that can do in seconds what once required specialized cameras, optical equipment, and extensive film processing.

But what's impressive is how much of the underlying idea has stayed the same.

Vlahos didn't invent green screens, but he helped turn compositing into something filmmakers could use much more effectively and convincingly.

And considering how much modern filmmaking relies on combining real footage with computer-generated environments, it's pretty incredible how much of that history can be traced back to the work of one engineer... who didn't even have a film background.
