One of the biggest challenges Vlahos faced came during the production of *Ben-Hur*, released in 1959.

MGM was filming on 65mm film, which was much larger than the standard 35mm format. This created a problem because Vlahos had already been developing a specialized sodium-vapour camera, but it wasn't compatible with the larger format.

So, he needed another solution.

Vlahos developed what became known as the colour-difference travelling matte process.

Now, he didn't actually invent colour keying. As we saw earlier, filmmakers had already been using blue screens for years. What Vlahos developed was a more effective way of separating actors from those backgrounds.

His process used differences between the red, green, and blue information recorded on film to create more accurate mattes while preserving the actor's original colours.

You can see the basic idea here in the INTERACTIVE EXHIBIT 01.

What's interesting is that modern visual effects software still uses a similar principle. For example, tools like Keylight in After Effects or Nuke analyze the colour information in an image to create a transparency mask, allowing artists to replace a green or blue background.

The major difference is that Vlahos accomplished this using photographic film and laboratory processing, rather than computers and software.

His work helped lay the groundwork for the chroma-keying techniques we still use today.

But Vlahos was also developing another approach that worked quite differently: the yellow screen.
